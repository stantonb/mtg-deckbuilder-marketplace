The deck this is aimed at

What a 60-card Tidus build actually does, and the four seams in it

Tidus, Yuna’s Guardian is a {G}{W}{U} 3/3 legend. At the beginning of combat on its controller’s turn it moves a counter from one of their creatures onto another; its Cheer trigger draws a card and proliferates whenever one or more of their creatures with counters deals combat damage to a player, once each turn. It fronts the Counter Blitz Final Fantasy Commander precon, a Bant +1/+1 counters deck.

Squeezed into 60 cards, that becomes a creature-dense Bant midrange deck whose entire engine sits on one sentence: counters staying on bodies that connect. Four seams follow directly from it.

  1. Counters are cargo, and the ship is fragile. Every +1/+1 counter is riding on a creature. Kill the creature and the counters go with it — a removal spell on a countered creature is a two-for-one that no proliferate can undo.
  2. −1/−1 counters delete +1/+1 counters outright. Not shrink — delete. When a creature has both, they annihilate as a state-based action. Every blight effect in this pool is a permanent, uncounterable answer to the thing their deck is made of.
  3. Cheer needs damage to a player. A creature that is blocked and killed triggers nothing. Deathtouch blockers turn off the card draw and the proliferate at the same time, and they do it for two mana against creatures that cost far more.
  4. Their board goes wide before it goes tall. A deck spreading counters across several bodies folds to one symmetric shrink effect in a way a single fat threat would not.

The luckiest thing about this pool

The collection is built around High Perfect Morcant and the Lorwyn Eclipsed blight mechanic — a whole subtheme of putting −1/−1 counters onto creatures. Against a +1/+1 counters deck that is not a good matchup, it is a natural predator. Six cards in the pool make or move −1/−1 counters, and every one of them reads as removal against Tidus.

Decisions

Pool scoring, colors, and the mana

Why this archetype

The pool is a 100-card Commander deck: 69 distinct entries, of which 33 are basic lands and every single nonbasic is a 1-of. That fact drives the whole build. The four-of philosophy in the method assumes redundancy is available; here it simply is not, so this is a 60-card highlander: 36 singleton spells plus 24 lands, chosen for individual power rather than for consistency of drawing any particular card.

Scoring the 67 nonbasic cards by what they do, the pool is not close:

  • 15 cards remove or shrink a creature — 13 found by an oracle-text scan for destroy / exile / sacrifice / fight / −1/−1, plus Massacre Wurm and Nameless Inversion, whose “get −2/−2” and “+3/−3” wording the scan missed.
  • 35 creatures, 11 of them Elves, giving High Perfect Morcant a real critical mass.
  • 6 blight / −1/−1 cards: Blight Rot, Blighted Blackthorn, Bogslither’s Embrace, Creakwood Safewright, Darkness Descends, High Perfect Morcant.
  • 3 deathtouch creatures: Attercop, Ravening Warg, Trystan.

A pool with fifteen removal spells and a −1/−1 subtheme, pointed at a deck made of +1/+1 counters, wants to be interaction-first midrange: answer everything early, land a resilient threat, win the long game. That is the deck built here.

Why these colors

There was no choice to make. By color identity the 67 nonbasic cards are 25 green, 23 black, 14 colorless and 5 black-green — and nothing else. A third color does not exist in the pool, so the deck is B/G and the only real question was the ratio.

Black won the primary slot on the strength of its cards, not its count. Every unconditional answer in the pool is black: Bilbo’s Deadly Slice, Hero’s Downfall, Eaten Alive, Stir Up Trouble, Bogslither’s Embrace, Gnashing of Teeth, Blight Rot, Darkness Descends, Massacre Wurm. Green contributes bodies, fixing, and three deathtouch blockers. The final pip split is 33.5 black to 10.5 green — a 76/24 lean that the mana base follows.

A. Copy-count rationale

Every nonland card, and why it is the count it is

One reason covers 36 of 36 cards: the pool owns exactly one copy. The collection is a Commander deck, so every nonbasic is a singleton and validate_deck.py’s ownership gate caps each at 1. What follows is therefore not a count justification but an inclusion justification — why the card earned its single slot. Where a card would genuinely want to be a 4-of if the copies existed, the table says so.

Timing note: Darkness Descends is symmetric

It puts two −1/−1 counters on each creature, mine included, and my Attercop, Moonglove Extractor, Iron-Shield Elf and Scarblade Scout all die to it. Cast it when their board is wider or smaller than mine — which against a counters deck spreading a token swarm is most of the time — and hold it when I am ahead. It is deliberately the only symmetric sweeper in the 60.

The shortfalls are pool-bound, not a tuning error

The collection contains exactly one dual land (Overgrown Tomb) and one fetch (Evolving Wilds). With 22 basics and no other fixing, no split can satisfy both a double-black turn three and a single-green turn two — those two requirements need 31 sources between them out of 24 lands. Sweeping every split from 17/5 Swamp/Forest to 12/10 moved turn-three color screw only between 21.4% and 19.0%, bottoming out at the 15/7 split chosen here. Rebuilding green-lite (cutting Quarrel, Wood Elves and Champions of the Perfect) reached 16.6% — still above target, at the cost of three of the deck’s best cards. The mana is as good as this pool allows.

C. Sideboard guide

Intentionally empty

There is no sideboard. Kitchen table was the chosen format and no sideboard was requested, so the deck is 60 maindeck cards and nothing else. This heading is kept rather than dropped so its absence is a stated decision.

Worth knowing if that changes: the 31 nonbasic pool cards not in the 60 are all singletons, so a 15-card board would be 15 different cards. The natural ones against other archetypes would be Old Fat Spider and Boughside Wanderers as extra blockers, Warg Tactics as dedicated flier removal, Macabre Waltz and Dawn-Blessed Pennant for grind, and Selfless Safewright against sorcery-speed sweepers.

D. Legality statement

Format: kitchen table. No legality gate was applied — validate_deck.py’s kitchen format sets legality: None, and the run passed on size, copy limit and ownership only.

Normally the gate reads the cache’s legalities.<format> field. In this session it could not: the cache carries no legality data at all, because api.scryfall.com is blocked by this environment’s egress proxy and card_cache.py could not fetch. The legalities field is {} for every record, and the cache snapshot date is today’s build.

What this means for you: I cannot tell you whether this list is Standard- or Pioneer-legal, and I have not guessed. If you ever want to take it to a sanctioned event, re-run card_cache.py validate from a machine that can reach Scryfall and then validate_deck.py --format standard; that is a two-minute check and the only trustworthy answer. Card-by-card, the list draws on Lorwyn Eclipsed, The Hobbit, Modern Horizons, Foundations, Ravnica Remastered, Core Set 2020/2021, Lorwyn and Ninth Edition, so an eternal format is far likelier than a rotating one.

Card data provenance

The skill’s first iron rule is that card truth comes from the cache and misses are fetched from Scryfall, never from memory. Scryfall was unreachable here, and the repo’s bundled cache holds 199 cards from an unrelated Marvel demo collection — so all 69 names missed. Rather than build from memory, every card’s cost, type, power, toughness and oracle text was read from a web search against Scryfall, Gatherer and retailer listings for that exact card, then written into a cache in the toolkit’s own record format. card_cache.py validate then reported 69 names, 69 from cache, 0 fetched, 0 problems, and validate_deck.py, deck_audit.py and goldfish.py all ran against it as real gates. Two consequences: card text traces to cited web sources rather than to Scryfall, and there is no legality or price data. Spot-check any card that surprises you.

The three misses, and what was done about each

Color screw, 16.0% / 19.3% against a 10% target — acted on, then accepted. This drove the one real iteration (below), which cut it from 19.8% / 24.4%. Everything after that hit a floor: the land-split sweep never got below 19.0% at turn three, and a green-lite rebuild only reached 16.6% while costing three of the deck’s best cards. One dual land in a hundred-card pool is the binding constraint. Accepted, with the mitigation being four any-color rocks and dorks plus Wood Elves.

Curve-out through T3, 5.8% against a 30% target — not a real failure. This metric requires casting a spell of mana value n on turn n for three consecutive turns, so it is capped by the turn-one play rate, which is 32.2%. Reaching 30% cumulative would need eight to ten one-drops; the entire pool contains four playable ones and all four are in the deck. The meaningful number underneath it is on-curve turn two at 66.0%, which clears its threshold. A deck whose plan is to answer their turn-two and turn-three plays does not want a blank turn one filled with a bad creature.

Wincon first cast, T6.21 against a T4.5 target — accepted by design. The average is dragged by Massacre Wurm at six mana. Re-running with only the four- and five-mana threats named as win conditions barely moved it (T6.02 on the play), which is the honest signal: this deck spends its early turns casting removal, not threats. That is the point of an anti-deck. It wins on turn eight having answered six things, not on turn five having raced.

Sample games

Three narrated, using only cache-verified card behaviour

1. On the draw against a faster deck

Keep a seven of Swamp, Swamp, Forest, Eaten Alive, Ravening Warg, Blight Rot, Champions of the Perfect against an unknown aggro opponent. They lead on a one-drop and a two-drop; I have no turn-one play, which is the deck’s standing weakness. Turn two Ravening Warg walls the board — deathtouch means neither of their creatures can attack profitably into it, and they hold back rather than trade down. Turn three Eaten Alive with a land to spare handles their three-drop, turn four Blight Rot kills the creature they finally committed. By turn six Champions of the Perfect lands as a 6/6 that draws a card on every subsequent creature and the game is over on board.

Lesson  The deck has no turn one and does not need one. Deathtouch two-drops buy the tempo that a one-drop would have bought, and buy it against bigger creatures. The real risk on the draw is not their speed but drawing the green half of the deck with only Swamps — that hand kept because it had a Forest.

2. Against a sweeper on turn five

By turn four I have Scarblade Scout, Morcant’s Loyalist and High Perfect Morcant out, the Loyalist making the others bigger, and I am attacking. Their turn five is a wrath and all three die. This is the scenario the deck is least built for — there is no protection spell in the 60, because the pool’s only candidate, Selfless Safewright, grants indestructible, which does nothing against the counters this deck traffics in and was cut for it. What the deck does have is refills: Graveshifter returns Morcant to hand on turn six, Tom, Bert, and William comes down on turn seven as a 5/5 that returns itself from the graveyard when it dies, and Morcant’s Loyalist’s own death trigger already bought back an Elf on the way out. I rebuild one threat at a time instead of recommitting.

Lesson  Do not overextend into an untapped opponent with four or more mana. The deck’s answer to a sweeper is recursion, not protection, so play threats sequentially — one resilient body plus held-up removal beats three bodies into a wrath.

3. Against the Tidus deck

They open with a one-drop, put a counter on it, and add a two-drop. Turn three they cast Tidus and I let it resolve — there is nothing to gain by killing it before it has moved a counter. Turn four, at the beginning of their combat, Tidus moves a counter and they attack for Cheer; Attercop, held up since turn two, blocks the countered creature and its deathtouch kills it, taking both counters with it and denying the draw-and-proliferate entirely. My turn five is Blight Rot at the end of their next combat step on their best remaining threat — four −1/−1 counters annihilating its accumulated +1/+1 counters and leaving a corpse. Turn six Massacre Wurm gives their three remaining creatures −2/−2, kills all three, drains them for six, and leaves a 6/5. High Perfect Morcant follows and every Elf after it blights another of their creatures.

Lesson  Tidus is not the card to kill on sight — the creatures carrying the counters are. The Cheer trigger needs combat damage to a player, so a deathtouch blocker is a hard counter to their whole engine for two mana, and every −1/−1 effect is permanent removal of the resource their deck is made of. Save the unconditional removal for whatever they cast after the board is already clean.

Iteration log

One change, driven by an out-of-range playtest finding

v1 → v2 — swap two green creatures for two colorless fixers

Trigger. The v1 build passed validate_deck.py and every deck_audit.py range, but goldfish.py reported color screw of 19.8% on turn two and 24.4% on turn three against a 10% threshold, and only 22.8% of games had a turn-one play.

Change. Out: Lys Alana Dignitary ({1}{G}, whose mana ability only switches on with an Elf already in the graveyard — too conditional to be fixing) and Dawn’s Light Archer ({2}{G} flash reach 4/2, a good blocker but another green card in a deck that could not cast them). In: Giant’s Boulder ({1}, scry 2 then any-color mana) and Firdoch Core ({3}, any-color mana on a changeling Elf permanent).

Result. Color screw fell to 16.0% / 19.3%, turn-one play rate rose from 22.8% to 32.2%, and screw fell from 10.3% to 9.5%. Still outside threshold, so a land-split sweep and a green-lite rebuild were tested and both rejected — see the land-math note.
